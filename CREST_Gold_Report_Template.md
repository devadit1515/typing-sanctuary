# CREST Gold Award — Project Report Template

> **What this is.** A reusable skeleton for a CREST Gold report, kept so future projects start from a known-good structure instead of a blank page. Fill each section, delete the guidance blockquotes, and keep the numbering. Title page, Abstract and Table of Contents are *front matter* (unnumbered); the body sections are numbered 1–12. CREST does not mandate section numbers — this is a convention that reads well and maps cleanly onto the official assessment criteria.
>
> **Write for the real audience, not the rubric.** The best CREST reports read like a genuine document — a research write-up, a resource for a real organisation, something you'd be happy to submit elsewhere — that *happens* to cover the criteria because good work naturally does. Adapt, merge or rename these headings to fit the actual project (an ML project might make "Ethics" standalone and add an "Engineering challenges" section; a build project might not need a "Literature review"). Put the criteria-to-page mapping in the **Student Profile Form**, not woven through the report body, and don't narrate your own rigour ("the honest result…", "as required by the criteria…") — just do the work and let it show.

---

## Title Page

> Project title · your name · CREST Award level (Gold) · mentor name (or "independent — no mentor") · dates.

## Abstract

> One paragraph or two, ~200–300 words: the aim, the key methods, the main findings (with the headline number), and the conclusion. Write it last. Do not smuggle the full results discussion in here — one headline figure is enough.

## Table of Contents

> List every section and subsection with page numbers. Regenerate after the final export, because pagination shifts.

## 1. Introduction

> - **1.1 Background** — set the project in its field; what problem exists and why it matters.
> - **1.2 Aim** — one clear, testable sentence. Build the pass/fail test into it.
> - **1.3 Objectives** — break the aim into **at least 5** specific, measurable objectives (make-and-design projects). Give each a success condition you can actually check.
> - **1.4 Significance** — why the project matters and who it could help.

## 2. Literature Review

> Summarise existing research relevant to the project and locate the *gap* you address — synthesise across sources, don't summarise paper-by-paper. Use a consistent citation style (e.g. Harvard / author–date). Cross-reference the full list in §11.

## 3. Preparation

> - **3.1 Planning** — the approaches you considered and why you chose the one you did (a trade-off table works well).
> - **3.2 Resources and training** — datasets, tools, libraries, standards, people; any training or preliminary research you did to get ready.
> - **3.3 Timeline** — a dated timeline or Gantt-style table of planned vs. actual activity. Keep it minimal but real.

## 4. Methodology

> - **4.1 Approach** — the overall design and why it takes this shape.
> - **4.2 Materials and equipment** — what you used to run the experiment (hardware, dataset, key libraries).
> - **4.3 Procedures** — step-by-step build/method, in enough detail to reproduce.
> - **4.4 Experimental design** — how the evaluation is set up so the result is honest (splits, controls, metrics, repeats).
> - **4.5 Safety and ethical considerations** — safety protocols and ethical issues, with the decisions you made.

## 5. Results

> - **5.x Data presentation** — the data as tables, graphs and charts, labelled.
> - **5.x Observations** — the key patterns, stated plainly. Interpretation belongs in §6.

## 6. Discussion

> - **6.1 Data interpretation** — what the results mean against the aim and objectives.
> - **6.2 Comparison with the literature** — how your findings sit next to prior work.
> - **6.3 Implications** — for the field and the wider world.
> - **6.4 Limitations** — the bounds within which your conclusions hold.

## 7. Success of the Algorithm / Product

> - **7.1 Performance metrics** — how you measure success, and why those metrics.
> - **7.2 Results and robustness** — the performance figures; ablations / sensitivity checks that show the result is real, not luck.
> - **7.3 Comparison** — head-to-head against existing solutions or benchmarks.
> - **7.4 Challenges** — problems faced in development/testing and how you overcame them (problem → root cause → fix → how verified).

## 8. Conclusion

> - **8.1 Summary of findings.**
> - **8.2 Project success** — did it meet its aim and objectives? Be honest about near-misses.
> - **8.3 Future work.**

## 9. Personal Reflection

> - **9.1 Role and tasks** — what you actually did.
> - **9.2 Learning** — what you learnt (technical and about how you work).
> - **9.3 Challenges and improvements** — what was hard, how you handled it, what you'd do differently.

## 10. Acknowledgements

> Mentor and advisors; institutional support; anyone else who contributed. For an independent project, acknowledge the research community, library maintainers and standards you relied on, and disclose any AI assistance (with a pointer to the full statement).

## 11. References

> Full reference list in a consistent style. Include a pointer to any fuller provenance dossier.

## 12. Appendices

> Supporting material that would clutter the body: glossary, raw data, full result tables, extra figures, provenance fingerprints, and the AI-use statement.
