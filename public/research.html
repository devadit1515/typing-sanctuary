Can a computer verify
who is typing?

In part, and honestly. The model was built and measured on an ordinary laptop, judged only on people it never saw in training, and everything below re-runs from one command.

The result

Tested on the public CMU benchmark — 51 people typing the same password 400 times — against the strongest of the 14 hand-built methods from the 2009 study that created it.

Simple distance — the like-for-like comparison. The 2009 benchmark’s best method scored 9.6%.

14.2% EER

Blended verifier — three classical distances run inside the learned fingerprint. Won on all 14 test splits (p ≈ 0.0001).

10.2% EER

Uncertainty, stated — bootstrap 95% confidence interval on the headline, and the wider 14-split averages (18.7% / 13.3%) disclosed beside it.

[9.5, 19.2]%

The fairness finding — the easiest person to recognise erred under 1%; the hardest, over 33%. The same model can serve two people 37× differently, which is why this must never be the only lock.

37×spread

The model came close to the 2009 baseline and fell short. That shortfall is the finding. What did not exist before is the measurement itself: a learned fingerprint brought down to the small fixed-text benchmark, under an open-set protocol, with the stabilising component identified by ablation.

The method, briefly

Each keystroke contributes four timings: how long the key is held, and the gaps around it. A small encoder network — 83,505 parameters, trained in about 23 minutes on a laptop CPU — reduces one burst of typing to 128 numbers, its fingerprint. Training pulls the same person’s samples together and pushes different people apart, the same recipe face recognition uses.

Verification is classical statistics run inside that learned space: distance to your enrolled centre, to your nearest enrolled samples, and a variance-aware distance that knows how much your own typing naturally wobbles. Enrolment takes about a dozen samples and no retraining. On any failure the service asks for another factor; it never admits by default.

The evaluation is open-set: 35 people train the model, 16 are held out, and a runtime guard makes it impossible for a test subject to leak into training. The first version of the evaluation had exactly that leak, produced a flattering and worthless number, and was rebuilt — the report tells that story in full.

Check everything

Reproducibility is the contribution. The dataset is pinned by hash, the seeds are fixed, and the whole benchmark re-runs, asserts its own numbers and redraws every figure from one command.

ethics

Opt-in, minimal, fail-safe

Consent is revocable and opting out deletes the profile. The service stores the derived fingerprint, never what was typed. The reported model was trained only on the public, anonymised research dataset.
